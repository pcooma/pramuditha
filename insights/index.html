Insights

Clear answers for owners who want to grow

These are the questions that business owners across Sri Lanka ask most often and the answers below reflect the way I approach each one with real evidence rather than opinion. They are written for owners who would rather understand the reasoning than be handed a quick slogan.

Why is my business busy every day and still not clearly profitable?

Many owners work from early morning until late at night and still cannot say for certain which products or outlets or customers are truly earning the money. The reason is almost always that the numbers live inside a billing system or a pile of books and nobody has yet turned them into a clear picture.

Profit hides in the gap between what sells quickly and what sells at a healthy margin and those two things are rarely the same. A product can move in large volumes and still earn very little once you count the discounts and the returns and the real cost of serving it. When you look only at total sales you feel busy yet you cannot see where the money is truly made or lost.

The way forward begins by bringing every sale and every cost into one view so that each product and each route and each customer can be ranked by the profit it actually leaves behind. Once that ranking exists the picture usually changes within a few weeks because a small number of lines tend to carry most of the profit while another group quietly drains it away. From there the decisions become simple because you can lean into what earns and you can fix or drop what does not. This is work that pays for itself quickly because it rarely asks you to sell more and instead asks you to see clearly what you are already doing.

How do I reduce the cost tied up in my inventory?

Stock that moves slowly is one of the quietest ways a business ties up cash that it could use elsewhere. Every item sitting on a shelf represents money that has already been spent and that cannot be used again until the item sells.

The first step toward reducing this cost is to separate the stock that truly earns its place from the stock that only looks busy. Most businesses find that a large share of their shelf value comes from a small number of fast moving lines while a long tail of slow lines absorbs cash and space without giving much in return. When you measure how many days each item takes to sell and you compare that against the margin it earns you begin to see which lines deserve deep stock and which deserve very little.

From there you can set a sensible reorder level for each line so that you hold enough to serve demand without holding so much that cash sits idle. Seasonal patterns matter here because demand for many goods rises and falls across the year and ordering to a flat average leaves you short in the busy months and heavy in the quiet ones. A clear forecast built from your own history lets you order closer to real need and it keeps far less money locked in stock that moves slowly. The result is usually the same level of sales carried on a lighter and healthier level of inventory which frees cash for the parts of the business that grow it.

How do I decide the right quantity to import or order?

Deciding how much to import or order is one of the hardest calls an owner makes because both mistakes are expensive. Order too little and you lose sales and disappoint the dealers who depend on you and order too much and you tie up cash in stock that moves slowly and may expire or fall out of season.

The honest way to answer this question is to let your own demand history lead rather than a feeling about how the market might behave. When you study how each product has sold across past months and past seasons a pattern almost always appears and that pattern is the firmest ground you have for a forecast. A good forecast does not give a single number because real demand moves within a range and so the wiser approach is to model a likely low and a likely high and to order against the range rather than a single guess.

Lead time matters a great deal because the longer an order takes to arrive the more buffer you need to cover the wait and the more carefully you must plan ahead. The cost of money matters too because every unit you bring in early is money withheld from elsewhere until it sells. By weighing the history and the lead time and the cost of holding you can settle on a quantity that protects you from running dry while keeping far less cash trapped in excess. This turns a stressful guess into a calm and repeatable decision that you can make the same way every season.

How do I understand my true credit position?

Your credit position is the money the business is owed set against the money it owes and it is one of the clearest early signs of health or strain. Many owners feel the pressure of tight cash long before they can say exactly where it has gone and the answer is often sitting inside the credit they have extended.

When you sell on credit you are lending your own working capital to your dealers and customers and that lending has to be measured as carefully as any loan. The first step is to see the full age of every amount owed to you so that you know how much is current and how much has been outstanding for a long time. Money that has been owed for many weeks is far less likely to be collected in full and it quietly grows into a cost that never appears on any invoice.

At the same time you need the same clear view of what you owe to your own suppliers so that the two sides can be read together. When the money coming in is slower than the money going out the gap has to be filled from borrowing or from your own reserves. A clear credit view lets you set sensible limits for each dealer and it lets you act early on the accounts that are drifting rather than waiting until the strain is severe. Owners who keep this view current find that their cash feels far steadier even when sales stay the same because less of their money is quietly stuck in other hands.

How do I see the way demand spreads across different regions?

Demand rarely behaves the same way across every region and the differences carry real meaning for how you stock and sell. A product that moves quickly in one district can sit untouched in another because of income or taste or season or the simple strength of a local competitor.

When you only look at national totals these differences disappear into an average and the average hides both the opportunity and the risk. The way to see the real picture is to break your sales down by region and by product and by time so that each area reveals its own rhythm. Once this view exists you often find that a handful of regions drive most of the demand for certain lines while other regions favour an entirely different set.

This knowledge changes how you allocate stock because it lets you send goods to the places that will actually sell them rather than spreading everything evenly and hoping. It also changes how you plan growth because a region that already shows strong demand for a related product is often the safest place to expand next. Seasonal timing adds another layer because the busy months can fall at different times in different areas and matching your supply to those local peaks protects you from shortage and waste. When you understand how demand spreads you stop guessing about where to push and you begin to place your effort and your stock where the evidence already points.

How do I know which products sell best in each region?

Knowing which products are popular in each region is one of the most practical pieces of knowledge an owner can hold because it guides stock and promotion and growth all at once. The same catalogue rarely performs the same way everywhere and the reasons run deep through local habit and income and climate and the presence of rival brands.

When you rank your products by sales within each region rather than across the whole country the real preferences of each area come into view. You often discover that a line you consider minor is in fact a leader in a particular district while a line you push hard struggles there against a local favourite. This insight lets you tailor what you send and what you promote to each area so that your shelves match what people actually want to buy.

It also protects you from the common mistake of treating every region as a smaller copy of your strongest one because what works in a busy urban market may not fit a rural one at all. For growth this knowledge is especially valuable because the safest new products to introduce in a region are usually those that sit close to what already sells well there. By reading the regional winners you can plan promotions that land on real demand and you can avoid spending effort on goods that a particular area has quietly rejected. Owners who study this find that their marketing feels sharper and their stock feels lighter because every decision is guided by what each region has already shown it prefers.

How do I expand into new areas and strengthen my dealer network?

Expanding into a new area is one of the most rewarding moves a business can make and it is also one of the easiest to get wrong when it is driven by hope rather than evidence. The first question is never how fast you can grow but whether the demand you expect is actually there and your own data usually holds the first clues.

When a nearby region already buys a related product from you or when a neighbouring area shows the same profile as a district where you already do well the ground for expansion is far firmer. A sound entry plan begins by sizing the real opportunity and by understanding who already serves that area so that you enter with open eyes rather than a hunch. Distribution is the heart of the matter because a new market is only as strong as the network that reaches it and that means deciding whether to build your own dealers or to work through partners who already carry trust.

Each route carries a different cost and a different level of control and the right choice depends on the margin the area can support and the speed you are willing to fund. The order in which you enter new towns matters too because spreading yourself thinly across many places at once rarely works as well as winning one area firmly and using it to fund the next. A costed plan that sets clear priorities and clear targets turns expansion from a leap into a series of measured steps. The businesses that grow well are the ones that treat a new market as an investment to be proven rather than a bet to be placed.

How do I prepare a feasibility study a bank will take seriously?

A bank or an investor reads a feasibility study to answer one question which is whether the plan in front of them is likely to repay the money they put in. A proposal built on hope will rarely pass that test and a proposal built on evidence stands a far better chance even when the numbers are modest.

A strong study begins with a clear and honest view of the market because no plan survives if the demand it assumes does not exist. From there it sets out the costs in full including the ones that owners often forget such as working capital and the slow months and the money tied up in stock and credit before the business settles. The heart of the study is a realistic projection of cash because a lender cares less about profit on paper and more about whether cash will be there to meet each repayment as it falls due.

A credible projection shows not only the expected path but also what happens if sales come in lower than hoped because every lender wants to know that the plan survives a harder year. Sensible assumptions matter more than large promises and a study that explains clearly why each number was chosen earns far more trust than one that simply presents a confident total. When the risks are named openly and matched with a plan to manage them the reader sees an owner who understands the venture rather than one who only hopes it will work. A feasibility study prepared this way does more than secure funding because it also gives you a clear map of the business you are about to build.

How do I control dealer credit that is not being collected?

Credit extended to dealers is often the largest and the most fragile asset a distribution business holds and controlling it well can change the health of the whole operation. The money owed to you keeps climbing in many businesses and chasing it wears out the very people you need outside selling and serving customers.

The first step toward control is to see the full age of every amount owed so that you know at a glance which accounts are current and which have drifted far past their terms. An amount that has been outstanding for a long time is far less likely to be recovered in full and it quietly becomes a loss that no invoice ever records. Clear limits for each dealer are the next step because a limit set against a dealer's real capacity to pay protects both sides from a debt that grows beyond reach.

Collection works best when it is steady and early rather than harsh and late because a gentle reminder near the due date recovers far more than a hard demand after months have passed. Tying new supply to the settlement of old balances is a fair and quiet way to keep the cycle healthy because it rewards the dealers who pay and it gently restrains the ones who do not. A clear view of credit also lets you reward your most reliable dealers with better terms which strengthens the relationships that carry your business. When collection becomes a calm and regular routine rather than an occasional crisis the cash that was once trapped in other hands begins to flow back into the business where it belongs.
